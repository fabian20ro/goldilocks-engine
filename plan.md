# The Goldilocks Engine

## Master implementation plan

Status: implementation source of truth  
Initial product: Bedroom Developer to Local AI Laboratory  
Target platform: portrait-first mobile web/PWA  
Long-term destination: a pluralist, Culture-like post-scarcity civilization

---

## 1. Product thesis

The Goldilocks Engine is a portrait-first incremental systems game about building increasingly powerful AI infrastructure without letting optimization, incentives, fear, hype, or concentrated authority destroy the purpose of the project.

The player begins as a bedroom developer with one computer, limited savings, and a few hours each evening. They run local models, take freelance work, enter Kaggle-like competitions, publish open-source projects, attract creators, conduct research, and eventually found a small AI laboratory.

Future expansions may continue through startups, machine-managed organizations, industrial compute, automated science, a solar economy, and constitutional design for a post-scarcity civilization.

The central question:

> Can the player build something powerful without allowing its proxies, incentives, institutions, or public narrative to replace what they actually value?

The thematic pattern at every scale:

1. The player wants an outcome.
2. The player chooses a measurable signal.
3. An agent or institution optimizes that signal.
4. Reality diverges from the player's intent.
5. The player must diagnose, redesign, or live with the consequences.

Examples:

- Want a model that generalizes; optimize the public leaderboard; fail on the private leaderboard.
- Want useful software; optimize engagement; create dependency and support overload.
- Want productive teams; optimize visible ticket completion; create meaningless tickets and hidden defects.
- Want human flourishing; optimize a scalar proxy; produce wireheading, stagnation, dictatorship, or paperclips.

## 2. Product strategy

### 2.1 First release

The first release is a complete, replayable game covering:

> Bedroom Developer -> Independent Builder -> First Recognition -> Local AI Laboratory

It must stand alone. The project must not rely on future startup, Dyson-sphere, or ASI content to become enjoyable.

### 2.2 Expansion strategy

Later stages extend the same simulation grammar; they are not separate games attached to a prologue.

Every expansion preserves the same decisions:

- Throughput versus resilience
- Specialization versus flexibility
- Automation versus observability
- Growth versus reversibility
- Measurement versus reality
- Centralization versus contestability
- Immediate output versus retained knowledge

### 2.3 Primary kill gate

Before narrative depth, research characters, labor politics, or cosmic content:

> Reconfiguring a constrained vertical pipeline must be enjoyable for at least thirty minutes.

If the pipeline toy is not fun, redesign it before adding systems or content.

### 2.4 Scope rule

The first release ends at the founding of a local laboratory. Startup departments, workforce politics, state competition, automated science, orbital industry, and post-scarcity governance remain design constraints and expansion targets—not launch requirements.

Each milestone is independently gated. A later milestone may not be used to compensate for a failed earlier gate.

Owner-directed experiment note (2026-07-16): the original human gates and their evidence history remain unchanged, but the owner has authorized the bounded **Workstation Expansion I** progression slice defined under Milestone 2 and D-007 without structured telemetry. This waiver permits implementation and independent automated verification; it is not a claim that either human gate passed.

## 3. Non-negotiable design constraints

- Portrait-first; never require phone rotation.
- One-handed play; large touch targets; no required pinch zoom.
- The player remains “inside the device” throughout the game.
- The interface evolves without abandoning its visual grammar.
- The central interaction: build, route, observe, and improve pipelines.
- Old mechanics become automated at higher abstraction levels.
- Idle progress comes from player-authored policies, not mere waiting.
- No single alignment, safety, fear, trust, or worker-anger meter.
- Signals and measured values may differ from underlying reality.
- Research is uncertain, personnel-driven, and useful even when it fails.
- Hype and fear are different economic forces.
- Workers and opponents are agents with specific interests, not generic obstacles.
- Failure is legible without false causal certainty.
- Meta-progression improves information, options, and variety—not universal production multipliers.
- Real researchers and creators require permission or licensing; base content uses original characters.
- Durable technical principles outrank transient products, frameworks, and model brands.

## 6. Moment-to-moment loop

The short loop lasts thirty seconds to several minutes.

1. **Observe** — identify a bottleneck, anomaly, opportunity, queue, failure, or warning.
2. **Modify** — replace a module, reroute work, change a policy, allocate compute, or queue an experiment.
3. **Run** — watch resources flow, queues form, jobs complete, and failures propagate.
4. **Interpret** — decide whether apparent improvement is real progress, noise, leakage, overfitting, or a workload tradeoff.
5. **Automate** — delegate understood work through bounded policies.
6. **Escalate** — encounter a new bottleneck one abstraction level higher.

For accepted work, the choice is durable at queue time: each task records its workload identity and gross quote, while actual operating cost remains a consequence of the configuration that processes it. Current demand and quote must be visible before queuing. Repeating one workload saturates its demand and lowers later quotes; simulated time recovers neglected demand. This creates a rotate-or-specialize decision without opaque payout changes.

A useful five-minute phone session:

1. Read the offline report.
2. Investigate one unexpected result.
3. Reconfigure one pipeline or policy.
4. Queue one or two jobs.
5. Resolve one opportunity or warning.
6. Leave the system operating within chosen limits.

## 7. First-release progression

### 7.1 Stage A — Bedroom Developer

Starting conditions:

- One modest computer
- Limited salary and savings
- Several free hours per evening
- Basic programming skill
- No public reputation
- A rapidly changing AI news environment

Initial main-screen resources:

- Money
- Available time
- Compute capacity
- Thermal/energy headroom
- Reputation

Activities:

- Run local models
- Test quantization
- Accept freelance work
- Learn durable technical concepts
- Build small tools
- Enter one competition
- Publish experiments
- Contact creators
- Upgrade hardware

Teaching principle: create the problem before explaining the concept.

| Player pain | Concept introduced |
| --- | --- |
| Model does not fit | Quantization |
| Long context slows output | Context and KV-cache cost |
| GPU sits idle | Batching and utilization |
| Fast model performs poorly | Quality-efficiency tradeoff |
| Public score rises; private score falls | Overfitting and validation |
| Demo works; users fail | Distribution shift |
| Expensive upgrade barely helps | System bottlenecks |

Exit condition: stable progress toward a repeatable source of income or technical reputation.

### 7.2 Stage B — Independent Builder

The player maintains competing workloads:

- Competition experiments
- Freelance jobs
- Open-source maintenance
- Product development
- Evaluation
- Public demonstrations

Funding paths:

#### Competition-first

- Strengths: evaluation skill, technical reputation, prize opportunities.
- Risks: overfitting, narrow techniques, uncertain income, weak product instincts.

#### Product-first

- Strengths: revenue, real users, product knowledge.
- Risks: support load, reliability failures, deadlines, neglected research.

#### Creator-first

- Strengths: rapid attention, donations/sales, distribution, community.
- Risks: expectation debt, curated demos, support catastrophe, volatile attention.

#### Open-source-first

- Strengths: legitimacy, contributors, recruitment, external review.
- Risks: maintainer exhaustion, weak monetization, forks, governance conflict.

#### Freelance-first

- Strengths: predictable money, real problems, low public risk.
- Risks: client dependency, burnout, weak reputation growth, little research time.

No path is compulsory. Hybrid strategies incur coordination costs.

Exit condition: produce something independently valuable, not merely impressive under a selected metric.

### 7.3 Stage C — First Recognition

Recognition can come from:

- A strong competition result
- An open-source tool
- A local-model optimization
- A viral demonstration
- A useful product

New systems:

- Audience-specific reputation
- Creator coverage
- Expectations
- First research opportunities
- First recurring rival or collaborator

Audiences:

- Developers
- Researchers
- AI enthusiasts
- Entrepreneurs
- Customers
- Skeptics

One event may improve standing with one audience while reducing it with another.

Exit condition: convert attention or evidence into lasting capability without losing continuity.

### 7.4 Stage D — Local AI Laboratory

The player moves from individual experiments to institutional capacity:

- Multiple machines
- Parallel pipelines
- First hired or collaborating researcher
- Reproducible evaluations
- Multiple research hypotheses
- Documentation and retained knowledge
- Early laboratory culture

Final transition choice:

- Remain an independent laboratory
- Form a startup
- Build an open research collective
- Join or collaborate with a larger organization

First-release positive ending: a sustainable, technically credible laboratory.

## 8. Pipeline interaction model

### 8.1 Layout

Use a constrained vertical pipeline with limited branches, not an unrestricted 2D factory map.

Player interactions:

- Drag a module into a compatible slot
- Replace or bypass a module
- Reorder compatible modules
- Split or merge a flow at defined junctions
- Allocate compute, memory, time, or budget
- Inspect bottlenecks
- Pause jobs
- Save configurations as policies or presets

Workstation Expansion I capacity rules:

- Begin with source, three process positions, sink, and the defined shadow branch.
- A data-driven, exact-once purchase expands the same active pipeline to at least six usable process positions.
- Purchased positions begin empty/bypassed; purchasing never auto-buys, auto-fills, or duplicates modules.
- Empty/bypassed positions are valid. All unlocked positions accept compatible owned modules through tap, keyboard selection, replacement/reorder, and touch drag.
- Pipeline metrics and bottleneck feedback use the complete ordered active graph.
- Presets save and restore their honest topology/capacity; old or malformed topology recovers safely.

### 8.2 Module properties

- Throughput
- Capacity
- Latency
- Cost
- Memory use
- Quality effect
- Reliability
- Maintenance
- Observability

Only decision-relevant properties appear by default.

### 8.3 Visual feedback

Show:

- Queues above bottlenecks
- Memory pressure
- Thermal throttling
- Rejected or malformed data
- Job acceleration after upgrades
- Error propagation
- Unstable modules
- Branch traffic
- Predicted versus observed output

The pipeline feels alive through cards, icons, motion, and sound without requiring 3D.

## 9. Workloads

Initial workloads:

- Interactive chat
- Code generation
- Document summarization
- Batch classification
- Competition training
- Evaluation
- Small fine-tune
- Agent tool use

Workstation Expansion I stages these as at least eight durable, data-driven workload concepts. Four are initially available; at least four later workloads unlock through deterministic progress or capability requirements. A locked card names the exact requirement and current progress. Unlock progress persists and migrates without loss.

Each workload exposes before acceptance:

- Current gross quote and bounded deterministic demand/trend
- Queue-time quote locking
- Estimated operating cost and estimated net, or an honest uncertainty range
- A mechanical explanation for saturation/recovery
- An explicit warning when cost approaches, equals, or exceeds quote

Successful completions saturate only that workload and lower future quotes. Unrun demand recovers gradually with simulated time; no workload remains permanently dead. Accepted tasks keep their locked quote even if later demand changes. Negative-margin work remains an explicit player choice and is never auto-queued.

Different workloads stress different properties:

- Chat values latency.
- Batch evaluation values throughput.
- Long-document work stresses memory.
- Tool use stresses reliability and validation.
- Competition training consumes compute for uncertain gains.

Recurring choice:

> Maintain one flexible pipeline or several specialized pipelines?

No universally best configuration.

## 10. Economy and attention

Early scarce resources:

- Money
- Personal hours
- Hardware capacity
- Electricity
- Sleep/energy
- Public attention

Example allocation:

```text
Competition experiments  1.5 h
Freelance work           1.5 h
Learning                 0.5 h
Maintenance              0.5 h
Evaluation               0.0 h
```

Growth engines create new constraints:

- More compute -> more power, heat, and maintenance.
- More users -> more support and reliability work.
- More attention -> more expectation debt.
- More automation -> less direct visibility.
- More collaborators -> coordination and culture costs.
- More specialization -> less flexibility.

Prefer bottlenecks, uncertainty, maintenance, trust, and opportunity cost over arbitrary walls.

The bounded early market must not generate money from waiting alone. Repeated single-workload farming eventually reaches nonpositive expected margin; rotating work and allowing time recovery restore viable alternatives. There is no universally dominant workload, and at least one profitable path remains available in early and midgame play.

Traits:

- Depth
- Taste
- Execution
- Mentorship
- Integrity
- Influence

- Autonomy
- Publication
- Open source
- Product impact
- Safety work
- Theory/experiment balance

> First-Principles Reconstruction — rebuild a simplified pipeline to reveal assumptions, redundant modules, technical debt, and missing understanding.

### 12.5 Team chemistry and knowledge retention

- Complementary theory, systems, data, and evaluation skills

- Shared technical language
- Leadership ambiguity
- Research/engineering separation
- Mentorship capacity
- Hero dependency
- Publication pressure
- Psychological safety for negative results

Project knowledge is divided among code/tests, documentation, distributed team knowledge, and tacit individual knowledge. Departure risk depends on where that knowledge resides. Education and mentorship convert fragile tacit knowledge into institutional capability.

## 13. Hype, fear, and creators

### 13.1 Hype

Promise:

> Use this and become richer, more productive, or historically important.

Produces attention, users, applicants, investment, community activity, and expectation debt.

### 13.2 Fear

Promise:

> Adopt now or become obsolete.

Produces tool purchases, impulsive upgrades, career switching, executive panic, hiring freezes, resistance, burnout, and race dynamics.

Fear dimensions:

- Displacement fear
- Obsolescence fear
- Control fear
- Race fear
- Catastrophe fear
- Bubble/fraud fear

### 13.3 Narrative objects

Each major narrative contains:

- Claim
- Source archetype
- Target audiences
- Deadline
- Evidence strength
- Emotional intensity
- Reach
- Beneficiaries
- Counterevidence
- Active effects
- Resolution rules

At its deadline, a claim may be correct, partly correct, wrong, delayed, self-fulfilling, reinterpreted, or forgotten.

### 13.4 Creator archetypes

First release:

- **Developer tastemaker** — usability, developer experience, latency, price, reliability.
- **AI-news amplifier** — broad implications, labor, AGI narratives, investment attention.
- **Builder-opportunity creator** — products, tutorials, small businesses, rapid adoption.
- **Skeptic** — benchmarks, failed demos, exaggerated claims, independent scrutiny.

Later: countdown prophet, survivor coach, labor advocate, acceleration evangelist.

Creators choose coverage based on audience incentives, preferences, access, usefulness, trust, and current narratives. They are not purchasable ad slots.

## 14. Expectation debt

Expectation debt: gap between public expectation and demonstrated capability.

Consequences:

- Launch pressure
- Lower tolerance for delay
- Stronger backlash
- Internal stress
- Incentive to manipulate metrics
- Difficulty publishing negative results
- Selection for aggressive investors, employees, users, and partners

The selection effect is the deepest cost. A hype-heavy project gradually fills with actors who require continued escalation, making repair slow.

## 15. Workforce and AI management — future expansion rules

Do not implement in the first release; preserve compatible agent and policy models.

Future workforce state:

- Technical confidence
- AI proficiency
- Leadership trust
- Professional autonomy
- Job security
- Experiment safety
- Workload
- Identity disruption

Resistance arises from concrete policies and incidents: surveillance, unreliable tooling, job threats, automated performance scoring.

Model workforce positions on two independent axes:

- Belief in AI capability: low to high
- Acceptance of AI authority: low to high

This permits technically sophisticated opposition: an employee may believe a system is extremely capable while rejecting its right to manage, surveil, or evaluate people.

Resistance forms:

- Quiet non-adoption
- Shadow workflows
- Metric gaming
- Withholding tacit knowledge
- Departure
- Petition
- Strike
- Whistleblowing
- Forking

Key principle:

> Intelligence or technical capability does not imply legitimate authority.

AI managers initially improve coordination while creating proxy gaming, suppressed uncertainty, hidden technical debt, and loss of appeal rights.

## 16. Failure and endings

First release target: five to eight substantial endings.

### Failure tiers

- **Setback** — recoverable incident inside the current strategy: failed run, invalid submission, hardware fault, lost customer, corrupt checkpoint.
- **Stage failure** — forces a major pivot or ends the current attempt at a stage: bankruptcy, disqualification, trust collapse, unsustainable burnout.
- **Run ending** — closes the run with a postmortem and unlocks: one of the named failure or success outcomes below.

Routine variance should create setbacks. Endings require an accumulated causal pattern, an explicit irreversible choice, or ignored/escalating warnings—not a single opaque random roll.

### Public Leaderboard Hero

Visible benchmark optimization; private test collapse.

### Viral Support Catastrophe

Attention and users arrive before reliability and support capacity.

### Hardware Debt Spiral

Repeated purchases without solving the actual bottleneck.

### The Tutorial Loop

Continuous migration between models and frameworks; no finished work.

### Maintainer Exhaustion

Open-source success without sustainable funding or governance.

### The Panic Business

No reliable automation; a profitable business built around repeatedly predicting the end of developers.

### The Invisible Laboratory

Excellent research without funding, distribution, or institutional continuity.

### The Honest Foundation

Positive ending: a sustainable, credible, independent local AI laboratory.

Failures produce an immediate new-run hypothesis, not arbitrary punishment.

## 17. Causal postmortems

The causal ledger records only what the simulation supports.

Every postmortem distinguishes:

- **Direct causes** — mechanically necessary causes.
- **Contributing factors** — conditions increasing likelihood or severity.
- **Correlated conditions** — nearby events without established causality.
- **Player-visible hypotheses** — explanations requiring more evidence.
- **Unknowns** — information unavailable with current instrumentation.

Example:

```text
Direct cause
Required memory exceeded available memory.

Contributing factor
Viral growth increased simultaneous requests.

Hypothesis
Reduced evaluation coverage may have hidden the issue.

Unknown
Telemetry cannot identify which workload mix triggered the peak.
```

The game may explicitly report insufficient evidence.

## 19. Offline progression

Offline progress uses three policy levels.

### Safe operating envelope

Continue only validated, conservative work. Slow; low risk.

### Delegated policy

Authorize bounded choices:

- Spending cap
- Retry count
- Allowed job risk
- Minimum evaluation coverage
- Resource reserve

### Escalation threshold

Pause for:

- Major purchases
- Public launches
- Irreversible publication
- High-risk experiments
- Reputation-sensitive responses

Return report:

- Completed jobs
- Resource changes
- Deferred decisions
- Incidents
- New evidence
- Policy actions and rationale

No unforeseeable offline event should destroy a run.

## 24. Simulation architecture

### 24.1 Determinism

A run is reproducible from:

- Game version
- Content version
- Seed
- Initial scenario
- Ordered player commands

### 24.2 Time model

Hybrid simulation:

- Continuous or analytical flows for stable production
- Discrete events for completion and incidents
- Scheduled checkpoints for market and narrative change

Do not simulate every offline second.

Workstation Expansion I exposes exact 1×, 4×, 16×, and 64× controls. They advance the same fixed bounded simulation quanta and must be outcome-equivalent for the same elapsed simulated time and ordered commands. Time speed remains separate from animation and processing pause.

### 24.3 Update order

Proposed initial order:

1. Apply queued player commands.
2. Resolve production and consumption.
3. Advance jobs and experiments.
4. Resolve completed evaluations/research.
5. Update narratives and audience beliefs.
6. Update relationships and agent decisions.
7. Generate incidents from eligible conditions.
8. Evaluate progression and ending conditions.
9. Append causal events.

The order must be explicit, documented, and covered by deterministic tests.

### 24.4 Versioned state

State includes:

- Resources
- Pipeline graph
- Jobs
- Policies
- Research frontier
- Narrative state
- Relationships
- Progression state
- Event references
- RNG state
- Schema/content versions
- Purchased/active pipeline capacity and complete topology

- Product-first viability

### Milestone 1 — Pipeline toy

- Queues

- Configuration comparison

Exit gate:

Failure action: redesign; do not proceed by adding content.

### Milestone 2 — Bedroom vertical slice

Add:

- Personal schedule
- Savings and electricity
- Hardware purchases
- Local-model workloads
- Freelance work
- Quantization
- First competition
- First product
- Save/load
- Basic offline policies

Owner-authorized bounded first slice — **Workstation Expansion I**:

- Purchasable expansion from three to at least six process positions in one active pipeline
- At least eight staged workloads: four initial, at least four deterministic later unlocks with visible requirement/progress
- Per-task workload identity, queue-time locked gross quote, configuration-dependent actual cost, and visible settlement net
- Bounded deterministic demand/quote fluctuation, completion saturation, and simulated-time recovery
- Clear waiting tasks without clearing a partially processed active task, payout, refund, or demand mutation
- Fixed deterministic 1×/4×/16×/64× time
- Bottom-navigation-only global routing and progressive disclosure for portrait density
- Versioned migration from schema-v4/content-v3 saves and existing presets

This slice explicitly excludes researchers/characters, newer transient model brands, hype/fear/attention/reputation economy expansion, multiple or parallel pipelines, narrative, startup, labor, and later systems.

Executable acceptance for this slice:

- Preserve first meaningful module by five successful starter jobs and first alternate rig by fifteen successful jobs including that module purchase.
- Competent default expansion purchase: about 8–16 simulated hours.
- Fastest valid strategy cannot own every rig, module, and expansion before 24 simulated hours; representative competent play can complete that catalogue by 72 hours.
- Repeated single-workload farming eventually becomes nonpositive expected margin; rotation/recovery restores profit; no workload is universally dominant; no idle-only money exploit.
- Clear-waiting is exact, transactional, and active-task preserving, including malformed, empty, reload, and offline cases.
- Unit/property/balance plus repository-pinned Playwright cover 320/393 CSS pixels, 200% text, 44 CSS-pixel controls, starter/expanded pipeline, keyboard/tap/real-touch drag, locks, queue identity, quote locking, demand, clear, time equivalence, migration, offline, reduced motion, screen-reader labels, no color-only meaning, bottom-nav-only routing, no duplicate page CTAs, recovery, and double activation.
- Real visual inspection at both portrait widths supplements, but does not replace, committed browser checks.

Exit gate:

> The stage feels like a complete small game rather than a tutorial for later content.

## 31. Expansion roadmap

### Expansion 1 — The Startup

Adds revenue, runway, customers, investors, launches, competitors, evidence, attention, legitimacy, and organizational culture.

Question:

> Can the organization scale without allowing perception to replace reality?

### Expansion 2 — Managed by Machines

Adds development teams, AI managers, worker factions, experiment safety, surveillance, strikes, whistleblowing, apprenticeship collapse, and worker governance.

Question:

> Can automation increase capability without removing the ability to contest automated authority?

### Expansion 3 — Compute State

Adds datacenters, chips, power, governments, regulation, national races, and compute concentration.

Question:

> Can cautious institutions survive competition from less cautious ones?

### Expansion 4 — Automated Science

Adds AI researchers, recursive improvement, capability jumps, independent evaluators, interpretability, containment, and assurance cases.

The assurance case replaces any universal alignment score. It presents uncertain, dated, and potentially conflicted evidence across:

- Goal stability under modification
- Evaluation coverage and distribution shift
- Interpretability
- Corrigibility and appeal
- Adversarial robustness
- Oversight capacity
- Evaluator capability gap
- Independent verification
- Reversibility
- Concentration of power
- Institutional legitimacy

Each claim records an estimate or range, evidence quality, last test, evaluator, conflicts of interest, and known blind spots. Passing every test can increase concern when the evaluated system understands or shapes the tests better than its evaluators.

Question:

> Can a system remain understandable and corrigible after it surpasses its evaluators?

### Expansion 5 — Solar Economy

Adds asteroid mining, orbital factories, self-replication, habitats, solar energy collection, and autonomous logistics.

Question:

> Can industrial autonomy remain bounded at irreversible scale?

### Expansion 6 — The Plurality

Adds post-scarcity governance, mind copying, exit rights, value conflicts, ASI authority, independent communities, and constitutional design.

Possible outcomes:

- Paperclip universe
- Wirehead garden
- Museum Earth
- Perfect dictatorship
- Competitive extinction
- Quiet stagnation
- Fragmented abundance
- Culture-like pluralist abundance

The constitutional game is organized around contested tradeoffs, not a list of morally correct upgrades:

- Coordination versus autonomy
- Reversibility versus speed
- Safety versus experimentation
- Central authority versus dangerous proliferation
- Stability versus the ability to revise values
- Universal guarantees versus community exit rights
- Human authority versus competence-based machine authority

Several futures must be genuinely attractive and internally coherent. The richest pluralist ending is earned through durable institutions and accumulated history, not by keeping a hidden ideology meter above a threshold. Some inefficiency is evidence that different lives and institutions remain possible.

Question:

> Can intelligence coordinate civilization without turning one vision of flourishing into compulsory optimization?

## 33. Risks and mitigations

### Compelling thesis, weak game

Mitigation: Pipeline Toy kill gate before narrative implementation.

### Multiple shallow games

Mitigation: every expansion reuses resources, processors, constraints, signals, policies, and agents.

### Didactic or predetermined endings

Mitigation: several attractive futures with genuine costs; pluralism as tradeoff, not checkbox.

### Failures become solved thresholds

Mitigation: interactions, dependence, shared blind spots, selection effects, and uncertainty.

### False causal confidence

Mitigation: distinguish cause, contributor, correlation, hypothesis, and unknown.

### Hype dominates optimal play

Mitigation: audience memory, internal reactions, slow trust repair, and stakeholder selection effects.

### Worker caricatures

Mitigation: concrete grievances and local incidents; no generic rebellion meter.

### Local-LLM tutorial ages quickly

Mitigation: durable constraints; replaceable brand/framework content.

### Portrait UI becomes a dashboard wall

Mitigation: strict main-screen rule and progressive disclosure.

### Idle play punishes absence

Mitigation: bounded offline policies and escalation thresholds.

### Research feels random

Mitigation: show evidence, strategic fit, reuse value, opportunity cost, and team competence.

### Content burden overwhelms development

Mitigation: pipeline-first scope; later content editor; systemic routine events plus handcrafted arcs.

### Late-game abstraction loses attachment

Mitigation: persistent people, institutions, artifacts, and consequences from the first stage.

## 34. Feature acceptance rule

Every proposed feature must answer:

1. Does it improve or meaningfully affect the pipeline?
2. Does it create a new decision rather than another number?
3. Does it reuse the universal simulation grammar?
4. Can it create both benefit and harm?
5. Can the player understand its consequences?
6. Does it fit the current milestone?

Reject or defer features that fail these questions.

## 35. Definition of success

The first game succeeds when a player can tell a story like:

> I began with an underpowered computer and entered competitions to fund an upgrade. I overfit the first competition, then built a better validation pipeline. A creator made my local coding tool popular, but support nearly ended the run. I used the attention to fund uncertain research, worked with a brilliant but difficult collaborator, and founded a small laboratory. One run failed because I chased every framework. Another failed because hype outran reliability. The successful run was slower, understandable, and sustainable.

The long-term series succeeds when that player can later say:

> The evaluation culture I formed in the bedroom stage shaped the laboratory, the company, and the institutions governing automated science. We avoided paperclips but nearly created a safe dictatorship. Civilization survived because automated systems remained contestable and communities retained the right to choose differently.

The solar economy is the spectacle.

The pipeline is the game.

The accumulated history of decisions is the product.
